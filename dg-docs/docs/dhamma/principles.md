# Principles of Translation

Principles and approaches applied wherever a translation or edit on Dhamma.gift is marked **"o"** — "translation o" (text fully translated from Pali) or "ed. o" (a text fragment amended against the Pali original) according to these Principles.

## In the Middle: Between Adaptation and Grammatical Analysis

Consistency and unified terminology make it possible to see the Buddha's teaching with greater depth and subtlety — something that literary, context-based translations cannot provide.

A literal translation functions as a necessary "bridge," allowing readers to perceive the original structure of the text and the logic of the Dhamma directly, with little or no immersion in linguistics. It fills a missing link for those seeking greater precision and independence in studying primary sources, but who have not yet learned Pali to the required level. This approach does **not** suggest that all translations should be done this way. Its aim is to fill the empty space between numerous literary adaptations and dense grammatical analyses.

## Principles

1. Any translation from Pali, or translation of another author's translation — whether a complete text, a text fragment, a dictionary entry, a topical article, or simply a term — in digital, printed, or oral form, is not considered final but is regarded as **"Work in Progress."** To obtain a final translation, meanings and terms must be correctly established. If such a translation is possible, it can only be done by an Arahant with the four "analytical knowledges" (<SiteLink to="/r/?s=pa%E1%B9%ADisambhid&q=an4.172#0.3">catasso paṭisambhidā</SiteLink>).
2. Terminology in the Buddha's Teaching must be **consistent** — this is an integral part of any adequate set of instructions or documents, let alone the Supreme Teacher and Supreme Teaching describing the most subtle, fundamental principles of the universe's workings and the methods of its realization. Dhamma is **not** fiction. Meanings and definitions are uniform across all texts, especially those concerning the Four Noble Truths and adjacent layers of text. More detail in [Rationale](/rationale).
3. If a concept has a definition in the suttas, that meaning is applied. Example: *dukkha* has a definition in the suttas — "Pain (dukkha) is bodily pain, bodily discomfort..." (*Analysis of Truths*, <SiteLink to="/r/?q=mn141&s=dukkha&lang=pli-rus#16.1">mn141</SiteLink>); see also [Rationale](/rationale). Ignoring direct term definitions from the suttas is a sub-optimal approach that renders the whole of the Buddha's Teaching not well-formulated (not *svākkhāto*).
4. If a word's definition is found neither in the suttas nor in the Vinaya, a **systematic translation** is preferred over a contextual one.[^1] The closest possible equivalent — a single word or a word cluster — is selected and applied consistently across all texts. More on the issues with contextual translation in [Rationale](/rationale).
5. If a definition cannot be found in the suttas but can be found in the Vinaya Vibhaṅga (hereinafter Vinaya), that meaning is applied.
6. If a word's definition is found neither in the suttas nor in the Vinaya, a systematic translation is preferred over a contextual one[^1] — ideally, one word that maximally covers all usage contexts across all or most occurrences in the suttas and Vinaya. More in [Rationale](/rationale).
7. If a word occurs too rarely, or only in monotonous cases, and has no definition in the suttas or Vinaya, a contextual or dictionary meaning is used.
8. If a generally accepted translation or interpretation — including one from a commentary or sub-commentary — contradicts the points above, that translation or interpretation is not applied.
9. If a Pali concept is broader than a single English concept, a word cluster (or a word from that cluster) is used. Example: *rāga* — passion-enchantment; *anicca* — unreliability, instability, untrustworthiness. Such a cluster may appear at the first occurrence in the text; afterward only the key word is used, as if it had been written: passion-enchantment (hereinafter *passion*). Or *jhāyatha* — ignite (ardently strive, concentrate, "meditate," contemplate), hereinafter *ignite*.
10. If a Pali concept is more specific while the English concept is broader, a clarification is used. Example: *ceto* — mind-thinking; *citta* — mind-state; *mano* — mind-representation, as if it had been written: mind-state (hereinafter *mind*).
11. Preserving the word order of the Pali sentence is preferred.
12. Preserving Pali grammatical structure is preferred.
13. Preserving the structure of Pali words with negative prefixes is preferred over antonyms. Example: *byapada* and *abyapada* — "hostility" and "non-hostility," not "hostility" and "friendliness."
14. Case endings are used only where the form without them sounds completely unnatural in English. Where possible, it is recommended to omit case endings within compound words,[^2] preserving their open, polysemantic structure — for example, Noble-Truth is both the truth of the Noble, and a truth leading to nobility, and one from the nobles, and one for becoming noble, and one for those already noble.
15. In cases of semantic divergence, translation should be based on the meaning of the Pali term — as with "false friends of the translator,"[^3] not by external similarity to English equivalents. More detail in [Rationale](/rationale).

[^1]: **Systematic Translation (Consistent Terminology)** is an approach strictly grounded in the direct definitions of terms found within the primary sources (the Suttas and Vinaya). The verified meaning is applied consistently and universally across the entire corpus. **Contextual Translation** is an approach where such definitions are sidelined, and the translation of a single word varies — sometimes drastically — from text to text, driven by tradition, the translator's stylistic preferences, or audience expectations. See [Rationale](/rationale) for a detailed breakdown.
[^2]: In addition to the standard understanding of Pali compound words (see e.g. [Pali Compounds](https://www.digitalpalireader.online/docs/pali/compound.htm)), it is proposed to take a broader view. As a dash in a date range ("1–10") is not equal only to "1 and 10," a Pali compound such as "ascetics-brahmins" need not mean only "ascetics and brahmins," but the whole spectrum of the spiritual sphere from ascetics to brahmins — as with the whole spectrum from joy to passion in *nandīrāga*, not only "joy and passion." Case endings are used only for words with no suitable compound form in English — so instead of "sufferingo-lamentationo-paino-discontento-despair," it is written "suffering-lamentation-pain-discontent-despair," although the compound reading is implied.
[^3]: False friends of the translator — words formally similar but with a partial or distorted overlap in meaning. Examples: *aggressive* — "energetic and also aggressive" vs. "only aggressive, unrelated to energy"; *sympathy* — "compassion" vs. Russian *симпатия* — "attraction."
